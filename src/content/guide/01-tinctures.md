Heraldry paints from a very small box. It holds two metals, five colours and a couple of furs, and together they are called the [tinctures](/glossary/#tincture). Their names are old French, which is why red is gules and nobody says yellow. Learn the nine names on this page and you can read the colouring of almost any [coat of arms](/glossary/#coat-of-arms).

## The metals

The two [metals](/glossary/#metal) are gold and silver. Gold is called [Or](/glossary/#or) and is painted yellow. Silver is called [argent](/glossary/#argent) and is painted white. Yellow paint and gold leaf mean the same thing in arms, and so do white and silver.

Or is written with a capital letter so that nobody takes it for the word "or". Every other tincture stays in lower case.

## The colours

The five [colours](/glossary/#colour) are red, blue, green, black and purple:

- [gules](/glossary/#gules) is red
- [azure](/glossary/#azure) is blue
- [vert](/glossary/#vert) is green
- [sable](/glossary/#sable) is black
- [purpure](/glossary/#purpure) is purple

Gules, azure and sable do most of the work in medieval arms. Vert is less common and purpure is rare. Heraldry does not fix the exact shade: any clear red is gules, and no herald will send a shield back for being a little too crimson. Later heraldry added a few muddier shades called [stains](/glossary/#stain), such as murrey and tenné. They are scarce, and the maker leaves them out.

```arms
Per pale Or and argent | The two metals: Or and argent
Tierced per pale gules, azure and vert | Three of the colours: gules, azure and vert
Per pale sable and purpure | The other two: sable and purpure
```

## The furs

A [fur](/glossary/#fur) is a pattern that counts as a tincture in its own right. The two oldest come from the linings of expensive cloaks. [Ermine](/glossary/#ermine) is the white winter coat of the stoat, sewn with the black tips of the tails: in arms, a white field with black spots. [Vair](/glossary/#vair) is squirrel skin, with blue-grey backs and white bellies sewn in alternating rows: in arms, rows of blue and white bell shapes.

Both have variants in other tinctures, which the maker does not draw yet. The dukes of Brittany bore ermine and nothing else, which is about as simple as arms can get.

```arms
Ermine | Ermine alone: the arms of the dukes of Brittany
Per pale ermine and vair | The two furs side by side: ermine and vair
```

## Proper

A [charge](/glossary/#charge) shown in its natural colours is called [proper](/glossary/#proper): a brown bear, a green tree with a brown trunk. It is a useful escape hatch and an easy one to overuse, because natural colours are often dull colours that blur at a distance. The maker keeps proper for one bird only, and leaves you to find it.

## The rule of tincture

Heraldry's best-known rule fits in one line: do not put a metal on a metal, or a colour on a colour. A gold lion goes on a red, blue, green, black or purple [field](/glossary/#field), never on a white one. A red cross goes on white or gold, never on blue.

This is the [rule of tincture](/glossary/#rule-of-tincture), and it exists for a practical reason. Arms were made to be recognised across a battlefield or a tournament ground, on a shield, a banner or a seal, by people who had seconds to tell friend from enemy. The metals are light and the colours are dark, so a metal against a colour always gives strong contrast. Red on blue turns to mud at fifty paces. Road signs keep to light on dark and dark on light for the same reason.

```arms
Azure, a bend Or | Metal on colour: the gold bend carries across a field
Azure, a bend gules | Colour on colour: the red bend sinks into the blue
Or, a lion rampant gules | Colour on metal: Scotland's red lion on gold, shown here without its border
Sable, a lion rampant gules | Colour on colour: the same lion, nearly lost
```

The maker checks the rule as you work. When two layers clash it names them in words, with an icon beside them, and offers a fix in one click.

## What the rule does not cover

The rule is about one thing lying on another. It leaves several cases alone, and so does the maker's check:

- The parts of a divided field lie side by side, so neither is on the other. A field may be split between two colours or two metals.
- A charge laid across a field of a metal and a colour cannot contrast with both halves, so it may be of any tincture.
- A fur holds a light and a dark tincture at once. It may lie on anything, and anything may lie on it.
- A charge that is proper goes where it likes.
- Small details are exempt. A lion's claws and tongue are often painted in a different tincture from the beast, and they are too small to matter.

```arms
Per pale gules and azure | Two colours side by side: a division, so no rule is broken
Per pale argent and gules, an eagle displayed sable | A charge across a field of a metal and a colour
Ermine, a chief gules | A colour on a fur
Gules, a bend vair | A fur on a colour
```

## Arms that break the rule on purpose

The most famous exception is the Kingdom of Jerusalem. Its arms were gold crosses on a silver field: one large cross with a small one in each corner. That is metal on metal, in some of the most prestigious arms in Christendom. The traditional explanation is that the holiest of kingdoms deserved arms that stood outside the ordinary rules.

Heralds have a name for arms like these: [armes à enquérir](/glossary/#armes-a-enquerir), "arms to enquire about". The breach is there to make you ask why, and the answer is supposed to be a good story.

```arms
Argent, a cross Or | Gold on silver. Jerusalem's own cross has crutch-shaped ends and four small crosses around it, which the maker does not draw
```

For an invented nation the lesson is simple. Obey the rule, unless your world has a story that explains the breach. Then break it once, and loudly. If the kings of Ostaril bear a silver sun on gold because a saint is said to have painted it that way, every herald in your world will know the tale.

## Tinctures without colour

Arms are often engraved, printed or carved where no colour is possible. Since the seventeenth century the answer has been [hatching](/glossary/#hatching), a code of dots and lines usually credited to the Jesuit Silvester Petra Sancta, who published it in 1638:

- Or is dotted
- argent is left blank
- gules has upright lines
- azure has level lines
- vert has diagonal lines that fall from your top left to your bottom right
- purpure has diagonal lines that fall the other way
- sable has upright and level lines crossed

Hatching is on the maker's roadmap. Until then, every swatch in [the maker](/) carries its name, so nobody has to tell the tinctures apart by colour alone.
