A [blazon](/glossary/#blazon) is a coat of arms in words. It is the arms: a painting is one artist's version of it, and any artist who follows the blazon paints the same arms. That is why a grant of arms is a paragraph of text, and why the maker shows you the blazon beside the picture and lets you type one in.

Blazon looks like a code and reads like one, but it has very little grammar. Once you know the order things come in, most blazons fall open.

## The order

A blazon describes the shield the way it would be painted: the bottom layer first, then each thing laid on top of it.

1. The [field](/glossary/#field): its tincture, or how it is divided.
2. The principal [ordinary](/glossary/#ordinary), if there is one.
3. The [charges](/glossary/#charge) around it, then the charges on it.
4. The [chief](/glossary/#chief) and what lies on it.
5. The [bordure](/glossary/#bordure), which goes round everything.

```arms
Azure | The field: a blue shield
Azure, a chevron Or | Then the ordinary: a gold chevron
Azure, a chevron Or between three mullets argent | Then the charges around it: three white stars
Azure, a chevron Or between three mullets argent, a chief ermine | Then the chief
Azure, a chevron Or between three mullets argent, a chief ermine, a bordure gules | Last, the bordure
```

## Four habits of the language

**The tincture comes after the thing it colours.** Blazon is half French, and French puts the adjective second: "a chevron Or" is a gold chevron, "three mullets argent" are three white stars. The field has nothing in front of it, so its tincture simply comes first: "Azure, …".

**Or keeps its capital.** The other [tinctures](/glossary/#tincture) are written in lower case. Or is not, so that nobody reads it as the word "or".

**Numbers are words, and one is "a".** "A lion", "two keys", "three crescents". The plural is the ordinary English one, with a few heirlooms: fleurs-de-lis, torteaux.

**What goes without saying is left unsaid.** A lion is rampant unless you are told otherwise. Three charges lie [two and one](/glossary/#two-and-one). A line is straight. A blazon mentions the unusual, which is how it stays short.

## Reading one, phrase by phrase

Take "Or, on a bend engrailed sable three bezants".

- "Or": the field is gold.
- "on a bend": the next thing mentioned will lie on a [bend](/glossary/#bend), the diagonal band.
- "engrailed sable": the bend has scalloped edges and is black. The line comes before the tincture.
- "three bezants": three gold discs, which lie on the bend because of that "on". A [bezant](/glossary/#bezant) is gold by definition, so no tincture follows.

Now "Per pale gules and azure, a lion rampant Or, a chief ermine".

- "Per pale gules and azure": the field is split down the middle, red on the [dexter](/glossary/#dexter) side and blue on the sinister.
- "a lion rampant Or": a gold lion, rearing, over the whole field.
- "a chief ermine": a band of ermine across the top.

```arms
Or, on a bend engrailed sable three bezants | "On" puts the bezants on the bend
Or, a bend engrailed sable between two torteaux | "Between" puts the charges either side of it
Per pale gules and azure, a lion rampant Or, a chief ermine | Field, charge, chief
```

## On and between

Two small words do most of the placing. "A fess between three crescents" puts the crescents on the field, around the fess. "On a fess three crescents" puts them on the fess itself. A blazon can do both at once, and then it names the outer charges first.

```arms
Gules, a fess argent between three crescents Or | Between: the crescents lie on the red field
Gules, on a fess argent three crescents sable | On: the crescents lie on the white fess
Gules, on a fess argent between three crescents Or, three torteaux | Both: crescents around, torteaux on
```

This matters for the [rule of tincture](/glossary/#rule-of-tincture). Each charge is judged against what it lies on, so a gold crescent beside a white fess is fine, and a gold crescent on a white fess is not.

## Pointing back

Old blazons avoid repeating a tincture. Instead they point back at one already named: "of the field" means the tincture of the field, "of the first" the first tincture mentioned, "of the second" the second. So "Gules, on a fess argent three mullets of the field" has red mullets.

It is elegant and it is a nuisance, because the reader has to count. Modern heralds mostly just repeat the word, and so does the maker. It understands "of the field", "of the first" and their cousins when you type them, and writes the tincture out when it answers.

## Common mistakes

- **Tincture first.** "A gold lion" is English. "A lion Or" is blazon.
- **Forgetting the comma after the field.** The field stands alone: "Azure, a bend Or".
- **"In pale" for "per pale".** [Per pale](/glossary/#per-pale) splits the field. [In pale](/glossary/#in-pale) lines charges up one above another.
- **Left and right.** [Dexter](/glossary/#dexter) is the bearer's right and your left. A beast faces dexter unless told otherwise.
- **Saying too much.** "Three mullets of five points, two and one" is just "three mullets".

## Writing your own

Start with the picture in the [maker](/) and watch the blazon change as you build. Then try it the other way: type a blazon into the box and see what it draws. The maker reads a deliberately small part of the language, the part it can draw, and when it meets a word it does not know it says which one. Real blazon goes much further, with hundreds of charges and a term for every way a lion can hold its tail. You do not need those to make good arms. Most of the best arms ever granted can be said in under ten words.
