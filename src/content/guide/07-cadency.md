Arms began as one man's mark. That raised a question as soon as he had sons: if the father's shield means "this is him", what do the sons carry? The answer is [cadency](/glossary/#cadency): the family's arms with a small, deliberate change, called a difference or a [brisure](/glossary/#brisure). The arms stay recognisable, and each bearer stays distinct.

For a worldbuilder this is a gift. A dynasty with cadet branches, rival cousins and a disputed succession can show all of it in its shields.

## The early way: change something

For the first two centuries there was no system. A younger son changed his father's arms in whatever way kept them familiar:

- a different [tincture](/glossary/#tincture) for the field or the main charge;
- a [bordure](/glossary/#bordure) round the whole shield;
- a [chief](/glossary/#chief) added, or a narrow bend laid across everything;
- small charges scattered on the field, or placed on the ordinary.

All of these still work, and the maker can draw most of them. They are the best choice when a branch of the family has become a house in its own right, because the change is big enough to see from across a room.

```arms
Gules, a lion rampant Or | The head of a house
Gules, a lion rampant Or, a bordure argent | A younger line, differenced with a bordure
Azure, a lion rampant Or | Another, with the field changed
Gules, a lion rampant Or, on a chief argent three crescents gules | A third, with a chief added
```

## The English system: nine small marks

Around 1500 the English heralds settled on a tidy scheme, usually credited to John Writhe, Garter King of Arms. Each son adds one small mark to his father's arms, at the top of the shield, by order of birth:

1. the eldest son: a [label](/glossary/#label), a bar with three tags hanging from it, which he removes when he inherits;
2. the second: a [crescent](/glossary/#crescent);
3. the third: a [mullet](/glossary/#mullet);
4. the fourth: a [martlet](/glossary/#martlet), a small bird drawn without feet;
5. the fifth: an [annulet](/glossary/#annulet);
6. the sixth: a [fleur-de-lis](/glossary/#fleur-de-lis);
7. the seventh: a rose;
8. the eighth: a cross moline, with split and curled ends;
9. the ninth: an octofoil, a flower of eight petals.

In theory a second son's third son puts a mullet on a crescent, and so on down the generations. In practice the marks become specks, and almost nobody goes past the second layer. The system is also voluntary: English heralds do not make anyone use it.

The maker has four of the nine as charges (crescent, mullet, annulet and fleur-de-lis) and cannot yet tuck one into the top of an existing design. A chief with the mark on it does the same job more boldly.

```arms
Vert, a chevron Or between three bezants | The father
Vert, a chevron Or between three bezants, on a chief argent a crescent gules | A second son, his crescent on a chief
Vert, a chevron Or between three bezants, on a chief argent a mullet gules | A third son, with a mullet
```

## The Scottish system: bordures

Scotland takes the opposite view. There, plain undifferenced arms belong to one person at a time, the head of the family, and every other member must have a differenced version recorded by the Court of the Lord Lyon before using it.

The usual difference is a bordure. The second son's line takes a plain bordure in one tincture, the third son's another, and later generations vary its edge with the [lines of partition](/glossary/#line-of-partition): [engrailed](/glossary/#engrailed), [indented](/glossary/#indented), [wavy](/glossary/#wavy), [embattled](/glossary/#embattled). The result is a family of shields that are plainly related and never the same.

This is the system the maker suits best, and the one that reads best at small sizes.

```arms
Azure, a saltire argent between four mullets Or | The chief of the name
Azure, a saltire argent between four mullets Or, a bordure Or | A second son's line
Azure, a saltire argent between four mullets Or, a bordure engrailed Or | A branch of that line
Azure, a saltire argent between four mullets Or, a bordure embattled argent | Another
```

## Royal labels

Royal families use the label for everyone, not only the heir. In the British royal family each child and grandchild of the sovereign has the royal arms with a white label, and the labels differ in the small charges placed on their tags: crosses, anchors, roses, hearts. The heir's label is plain.

## Children born outside marriage

Heraldry never had one mark for illegitimacy. Different countries and centuries used a baton sinister (a short diagonal bar from the bearer's left that stops short of the edges), a wavy bordure, or a bordure of alternating blocks. Often the mark showed royal blood as much as anything else, and families kept it with pride.

The "bar sinister" of novels does not exist. A bar is horizontal, so it cannot lean to the left or the right. The writers meant the [baton sinister](/glossary/#baton-sinister).

## Cadency for a fictional house

- Decide which system your world uses, or invent a third. A realm of strict heralds might number its sons with marks; a clan society might use bordures.
- Give each cadet branch one clear difference and keep it for generations. Readers will learn to tell the branches apart by their shields.
- Use a change of tincture for a branch that has broken away. A rival line that reverses the family's colours is making a statement.
- When a cadet branch inherits the main line, it drops its difference. That moment, the bordure coming off, is a scene worth writing.
