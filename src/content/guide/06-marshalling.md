Sooner or later two coats of arms need to share one shield. Two families marry. A bishop takes office. One king inherits a second kingdom. The art of putting several coats on one shield is called [marshalling](/glossary/#marshalling), and it is how arms tell history: a quartered shield is a family tree you can read at a glance.

The maker draws one coat on one shield and does not marshal two for you yet. This page explains how it is done, so that you can plan it, and shows the divisions that mark where the joins fall.

## Impalement

To [impale](/glossary/#impalement) two coats is to put them side by side, each squeezed into half the shield. The join runs down the middle, where a [per pale](/glossary/#per-pale) line would be.

It is used for two things. A married couple may impale their arms, the husband's on the [dexter](/glossary/#dexter) side and the wife's on the [sinister](/glossary/#sinister). And the holder of an office may impale the arms of the office with their own, the office taking the dexter side: bishops do this with the arms of their see.

Impalement is personal and temporary. It says "these two are joined for now". The children of the marriage do not inherit the impaled shield, and the next bishop impales his own arms instead.

```arms
Per pale gules and azure | Where the join of an impalement falls: down the middle
Gules, a lion rampant Or | The first coat, which would take the dexter half
Azure, three fleurs-de-lis argent | The second coat, which would take the sinister half
```

## Dimidiation, and why it was dropped

The first attempt at joining two coats was blunter. In [dimidiation](/glossary/#dimidiation) each shield was cut in half down the middle, and the dexter half of one was joined to the sinister half of the other.

The results could be splendid nonsense. The Cinque Ports of England joined the front halves of three lions to the back halves of three ships, and have borne lion-ships ever since. More often the result was simply confusing: half a bend looks like a different charge altogether, and half a chevron joined to half a chevron looks like one whole chevron belonging to nobody. Impalement, which keeps each coat whole, replaced it during the fourteenth century.

## Quartering

[Quartering](/glossary/#quartering) is the permanent version. The shield is divided into four or more parts, called quarters, and each holds a whole coat. Where impalement says "joined for now", quartering says "inherited".

The quarters are numbered along the rows, starting at the top on the dexter side: first and second across the top, third and fourth below. The most important coat goes in the first quarter. With only two coats to show, the first is repeated in the fourth quarter and the second in the third, so that the shield stays balanced.

In English practice a family gains a quartering when a man marries a woman who has no brothers and so is heir to her father's arms. Their children quarter both coats. Over the centuries a shield can collect dozens of quarterings, and a few families have displayed hundreds, which is a fine thing on a bookplate and useless on a battlefield.

Kingdoms quarter too. When Castile and León were united, their castle and lion were quartered on one shield in 1230, among the first quartered arms anywhere. The kings of England quartered the lilies of France from 1340 to show their claim to its throne.

```arms
Quarterly gules and Or | Where the joins of a quartered shield fall
Quarterly gules and Or, a lion passant guardant sable | Not marshalling: one coat, whose field happens to be quartered
```

## A division is not a marshalling

A shield split down the middle is not always two coats. "Per pale gules and azure, a lion rampant Or" is one coat with a two-coloured field and one lion across it. An impaled shield is two complete coats, each keeping to its own half, and its blazon says so: the first coat, then the word "impaling", then the second.

The test is whether anything crosses the line. If one charge sits across both halves, you are looking at a [division](/glossary/#division) of the field.

## The escutcheon of pretence

There is a third way. A small shield, an [escutcheon of pretence](/glossary/#escutcheon-of-pretence), can be placed in the middle of a larger one. In England a husband uses it to show the arms of a wife who is an heiress, "pretending" to represent her family. Kings have used it to add the arms of their own dynasty over the arms of the lands they rule.

## Marshalling for a fictional realm

Marshalling is one of the best worldbuilding tools heraldry offers, because it turns your history into something visible.

- **A royal marriage.** Impale the arms of the two houses on the day of the wedding. A generation later, if the bride was her house's heir, quarter them.
- **A union of crowns.** When one ruler holds two realms, quarter the two coats. Which realm takes the first quarter is exactly the kind of thing your characters would argue about for a century.
- **A merged state.** A federation might quarter the arms of its members, or choose a new coat with one charge from each. Calderwyn and Ostaril, united, could bear a lion and an eagle on one field.
- **A claim.** A ruler who quarters a neighbour's arms is announcing a claim to that throne. It is a declaration of intent that fits on a coin.

Until the maker marshals coats itself, make each coat here, download both as SVG and join them in a drawing program. Or take the simpler road that many real states took in the end: design one new coat, and let a [chief](/glossary/#chief) or a single [charge](/glossary/#charge) remember the old one.

```arms
Azure, a lion rampant Or, a chief ermine | Calderwyn
Or, an eagle displayed sable, a bordure gules | Ostaril
Azure, a lion rampant Or, on a chief Or an eagle displayed sable | A new coat for the union: Calderwyn's lion under Ostaril's eagle
```
