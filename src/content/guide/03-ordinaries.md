An [ordinary](/glossary/#ordinary) is a plain geometric band or shape that runs across the shield, usually from edge to edge. Ordinaries are among the oldest things in heraldry and still the strongest. A single band in a contrasting tincture can be recognised from further away than any lion.

## The nine the maker draws

- [Chief](/glossary/#chief): a band across the top of the shield.
- [Pale](/glossary/#pale): an upright band down the middle.
- [Fess](/glossary/#fess): a level band across the middle.
- [Bend](/glossary/#bend): a diagonal band that falls from the top left as you look at it.
- [Chevron](/glossary/#chevron): two sloping bands that meet in a point, like the rafters of a roof.
- [Cross](/glossary/#cross): a pale and a fess together.
- [Saltire](/glossary/#saltire): a diagonal cross.
- [Bordure](/glossary/#bordure): a border all the way round the edge.
- [Pile](/glossary/#pile): a wedge that comes down from the top edge to a point near the bottom.

```arms
Argent, a chief gules | A chief
Gules, a pale Or | A pale
Gules, a fess argent | A fess: the arms of Austria
Azure, a bend Or | A bend: the arms the Scrope family defended in a famous lawsuit of the 1380s
Or, a chevron gules | A chevron
Argent, a cross gules | A cross: the cross of Saint George
Azure, a saltire argent | A saltire: the cross of Saint Andrew
Argent, a bordure azure | A bordure
Or, a pile gules | A pile
```

The mirror image of the bend, falling from the top right, is the bend sinister. The maker can divide a field per bend sinister, but it does not draw the band yet.

## How wide to draw them

The old manuals give an ordinary about a third of the shield. No artist has ever held to that strictly, and none is expected to. A fess that carries three charges is drawn broad to give them room. A fess with charges crowding round it is drawn slimmer. The blazon never records the width.

What matters is that an ordinary looks like a bold band and never like a ribbon. A band that is clearly too thin has become something else, with a name of its own (see the last section). The maker sizes each ordinary to fit the shield shape you picked.

## Edges

The edges of an ordinary can follow any [line of partition](/glossary/#line-of-partition). The line's name comes after the ordinary and before its tincture: "Gules, a fess wavy argent". A fess wavy is the classic way to draw a river. A chief indented or a bordure engrailed makes plain arms a little less plain without adding a single charge.

```arms
Gules, a fess wavy argent | A fess wavy
Or, a chief indented azure | A chief indented
Argent, a bordure engrailed gules | A bordure engrailed
```

## Charges on and between

An ordinary organises everything else on the shield. Two small words do the work.

[Between](/glossary/#between) means the charges lie on the field around the ordinary. A chevron sits between three charges, two above and one below. A cross or a saltire sits between four, one in each gap. A fess, a pale or a bend takes charges on both sides.

"On" means the charges lie on the ordinary itself. They follow its direction: in a row along a fess, one above another on a pale, slanting along a bend.

[The rule of tincture](/guide/tinctures/) applies to each layer separately. Charges on the ordinary must contrast with the ordinary, and charges around it must contrast with the field. In "Or, on a bend sable three bezants" there are gold discs on a black band on a gold field, and every layer stands out from the one beneath.

```arms
Azure, a chevron Or between three mullets argent | A chevron between three mullets
Gules, a cross argent between four bezants | A cross between four bezants
Or, on a bend sable three bezants | On a bend, three bezants: they follow the slope
Vert, on a pale argent three torteaux | On a pale, three torteaux
```

The maker offers the numbers that fit each ordinary: three around a chevron, four around a cross, up to five along a fess or a bend.

## The chief and the bordure alongside

The maker gives a shield one principal ordinary. The chief and the bordure are the exceptions: either can be added beside it, or stand alone. In the blazon they come last, the chief first and then the bordure.

Both have long been used to add something to arms that already exist. In medieval Italy, families added a chief to show which side they were on: the Emperor's eagle for one party, the lilies of Anjou for the other. A chief can carry up to three charges of its own in the maker. A bordure is the classic way to mark a junior branch of a family, which the page on [cadency](/guide/cadency/) explains.

```arms
Argent, a lion rampant gules, on a chief azure three mullets Or | A chief with charges of its own
Azure, a fess Or, a bordure argent | A fess within a bordure
```

## Diminutives and sub-ordinaries

A narrower version of an ordinary is a [diminutive](/glossary/#diminutive), and each has its own name: a bar is a narrow fess, a pallet a narrow pale, a bendlet a narrow bend, a chevronel a narrow chevron. They usually come in twos and threes.

Beyond those lies a looser family of simple shapes called the [sub-ordinaries](/glossary/#sub-ordinary): the canton (a square in the top corner), the orle (a border set in from the edge), the pall (a Y shape), the billet (an upright brick) and more. Writers have never agreed on exactly which shapes are ordinaries and which are sub-ordinaries, so there is no need to lose sleep over the boundary. The maker does not draw diminutives or sub-ordinaries yet.

## Choosing an ordinary for your arms

One ordinary and one kind of [charge](/glossary/#charge) is a full design. Many famous arms have less.

Heraldry has no fixed dictionary of meanings, whatever some Victorian books claim. A chevron does not "mean" protection. That leaves you free to decide what a band stands for in your own world:

- a fess wavy or a bend wavy for the river a country grew up along
- a chevron for a mountain, a roof or a pass
- a pale for a road, a tower or a pillar
- a cross or a saltire for a faith, or for a crossroads
- a bordure for a wall, a coast or a ring of allies
