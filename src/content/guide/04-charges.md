A [charge](/glossary/#charge) is anything placed on the shield. The ordinaries are charges too, but the word usually means the pictures: stars, beasts, flowers, tools. Anything can be a charge. A small number of them have done most of the work since the twelfth century, and the maker has seven of those. Five are drawn in a plain style of its own. The lions and eagles are the work of heraldic artists, borrowed from Wikimedia Commons.

## Simple shapes

- A [mullet](/glossary/#mullet) is a star with straight-sided points. It has five unless the blazon says otherwise: "a mullet of six points". The name comes from the French for the rowel of a spur.
- A [crescent](/glossary/#crescent) is a crescent moon with its horns pointing up.
- An [annulet](/glossary/#annulet) is a plain ring.
- A [roundel](/glossary/#roundel) is a flat disc.

```arms
Gules, a mullet argent | A mullet
Azure, a crescent argent | A crescent
Sable, an annulet Or | An annulet
```

## The roundel and its seven names

English heraldry gives the roundel a different name in each tincture. The name already says the tincture, so the blazon does not repeat it.

- gold: a [bezant](/glossary/#bezant), after the gold coin of Byzantium
- silver: a [plate](/glossary/#plate)
- red: a [torteau](/glossary/#torteau), plural torteaux
- blue: a [hurt](/glossary/#hurt)
- green: a [pomme](/glossary/#pomme), French for apple
- black: a [pellet](/glossary/#pellet)
- purple: a [golpe](/glossary/#golpe)

"Or, three torteaux" is three red discs on gold. The maker writes these names for you. If you type "three roundels gules" it understands, and answers "three torteaux". A roundel of a fur has no name of its own and stays "a roundel ermine".

```arms
Or, three torteaux | Three torteaux: the arms of the Courtenay family
Sable, six bezants | Six bezants, which lie three, two and one
```

## Lilies, lions and eagles

The [fleur-de-lis](/glossary/#fleur-de-lis) is a stylised lily: three petals tied with a band. The kings of France bore a blue field strewn with gold ones, and cut the number to three in the late fourteenth century.

The [lion](/glossary/#lion) is the commonest beast in heraldry, and the [eagle](/glossary/#eagle) is the commonest bird. Kings liked the first and emperors the second.

A beast needs an [attitude](/glossary/#attitude), which is its pose. The maker draws a lion in twelve:

- [Rampant](/glossary/#rampant): rearing up on a hind leg, forepaws clawing the air. This is the usual pose for a lion, and what "a lion" means if the blazon says nothing more.
- [Passant](/glossary/#passant): walking, with one forepaw raised.
- [Statant](/glossary/#statant): standing, with all four paws on the ground.
- [Salient](/glossary/#salient): leaping, both hind paws down and both forepaws up.
- [Courant](/glossary/#courant): running flat out.
- [Sejant](/glossary/#sejant): sitting. [Sejant erect](/glossary/#sejant-erect) is sitting up with the forepaws raised.
- [Couchant](/glossary/#couchant): lying down with the head up. [Dormant](/glossary/#dormant) is the same with the head on the paws, asleep.

A beast normally faces [dexter](/glossary/#dexter) and shows its profile. One that turns its face to the viewer is [guardant](/glossary/#guardant), and the maker draws the rampant, passant and statant lions that way too. England's three lions are [passant guardant](/glossary/#passant-guardant), a pose that French heralds called a leopard.

Birds have attitudes of their own. An eagle [displayed](/glossary/#displayed) faces you with wings and legs spread, and nearly every heraldic eagle is drawn so. An eagle [rising](/glossary/#rising) stands with its wings lifted, about to take off.

```arms
Azure, three fleurs-de-lis Or | Three fleurs-de-lis: the arms of the kings of France from the late fourteenth century
Or, a lion rampant sable | A lion rampant: the arms of the counts of Flanders
Gules, three lions passant guardant in pale Or | Three lions passant guardant: the royal arms of England
Or, an eagle displayed sable | An eagle displayed: the eagle of the Holy Roman Empire, which later gained a second head
```

```arms
Vert, a lion statant Or | Statant
Gules, a lion salient argent | Salient
Azure, a lion sejant erect Or | Sejant erect
Or, a lion couchant gules | Couchant
Sable, a lion dormant Or | Dormant
Argent, an eagle rising sable | An eagle rising
```

## Why heraldic beasts look strange

A heraldic lion does not look much like a lion. It has a wasp waist, enormous claws, a tongue like a flame and a tail like a whip. Few medieval painters had seen a lion, but the main reason is practical. A charge has to be recognised at a distance and it has to fill its space. So the artist exaggerates the parts that identify the animal and stretches the body until it fills the shield.

The blazon fixes what is on the shield and leaves the drawing to the artist. That is why two paintings of the same arms can look quite different and both be correct. It is also good news for a worldbuilder: if you redraw the maker's lion in your own style, the arms are still the same arms.

One practical note. The maker's lions were drawn by Sodacan and are shared under the CC BY-SA 3.0 licence, so a picture with one of them in it should credit Sodacan and be shared on the same terms. The maker writes that credit into every picture it saves. The blazon is yours whatever you do with it.

## How many, and where

A blazon gives the number of charges, and their arrangement when it is not the obvious one.

- [Two and one](/glossary/#two-and-one): two above and one below, following the taper of the shield. Three charges lie this way unless told otherwise, so it usually goes unsaid.
- [In pale](/glossary/#in-pale): one above another, in an upright line.
- [In fess](/glossary/#in-fess): side by side, in a level row.
- [In bend](/glossary/#in-bend): in a diagonal line.
- [In cross](/glossary/#in-cross): in the shape of a cross.
- [In saltire](/glossary/#in-saltire): in the shape of an X, one in the middle and one towards each corner.

Take care with "in pale" and "per pale". The first lines charges up. The second splits the field.

In the maker, two charges always say how they lie. Three lie two and one, four lie two and two, five lie in saltire and six lie three, two and one, unless you choose another arrangement in Advanced mode. A field strewn with too many small charges to count is called [semy](/glossary/#semy), which the maker does not draw yet.

```arms
Azure, three mullets Or | Three charges lie two and one unless the blazon says otherwise
Azure, three mullets in pale Or | In pale
Azure, three mullets in fess Or | In fess
Azure, three mullets in bend Or | In bend
Gules, five bezants in cross | In cross
Vert, five plates | Five charges lie in saltire unless the blazon says otherwise
```

## Modern charges, and the tractor question

Heraldry never closed its list. Each age has put its own important objects on shields: castles and ships, then cannon, then locomotives, cogwheels and aeroplanes. Beside its seven classic charges the maker has about a hundred modern ones, from an anchor to a wizard hat.

So does a tractor belong on a shield? It can. Three questions decide it.

- Does it read as a silhouette? A charge is one flat tincture. If the shape is not clear in solid black, it will not work.
- Does it mean something to the bearer? A tractor is right for a nation founded on its collective farms. It is odd for a dynasty of sea lords.
- Will it age well? A tractor drawn today will look like today's tractor for ever. An ear of wheat says farming with no date attached.

Purists object to machines on shields. It is a matter of taste, and a tractor is no sillier than a lion on the shield of a family that never left Yorkshire.

```arms
Vert, a tractor Or | A tractor: honest, legible, and dated to the year it was drawn
Vert, three ears of wheat Or | Three ears of wheat say farming with no date attached
```
