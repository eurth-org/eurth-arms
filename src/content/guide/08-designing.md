Good arms look inevitable, as if the country could never have had any others. They got that way by being simple. This page is a method for getting there, for a country that exists only in your notes.

## Start from one idea

Pick one thing your nation would put on a shield. One. The river it grew up on, the beast its founder is said to have killed, the tool that made it rich, the star its sailors steer by.

The commonest mistake is to design by committee: a ship for the navy, a wheatsheaf for the farmers, a book for the university, a mountain for the north. That is a brochure. A [coat of arms](/glossary/#coat-of-arms) is closer to a signature. If your nation has four things to say, choose the one it would still be saying in five hundred years.

```arms
Azure, a ship Or | One idea: a seafaring nation
Azure, on a fess Or between three ships argent, three books gules | The committee's version: every ministry got something
```

## Keep it simple enough to draw from memory

The old test for a flag works for arms: could a child draw it from memory? The arms that have lasted eight centuries nearly all pass. A red field with a white cross. A black lion on gold. Three lilies on blue.

In practice that means:

- one [ordinary](/glossary/#ordinary), or one kind of [charge](/glossary/#charge), or one of each;
- charges in ones and threes, which fill a shield best;
- nothing that needs a label to be understood.

Arms were made to be recognised through a helmet's eye-slit, in the rain. Yours will mostly be seen at the size of a thumbnail on a wiki page, which is about as demanding.

## Say the name, if you can

[Canting arms](/glossary/#canting-arms) make a pun on the bearer's name, and heralds have always loved them. The kingdom of León bore a lion and Castile a castle. The city of Berne bears a bear. Far from being a joke at the bearer's expense, a cant is the surest way to make arms memorable.

Look at your nation's name. If it means something in its own language, or sounds like something, try that first. A country called Hohenfels wants a high rock. One called Brannoch, "place of ravens", wants ravens.

```arms
Gules, on a pile argent a mountain sable | Hohenfels, "high rock"
Argent, three ravens sable | Brannoch, "place of ravens"
Per fess wavy argent and azure, a tree vert | Aldermere: an alder over a lake
```

## Two or three tinctures, and obey the rule

Choose one [metal](/glossary/#metal) and one [colour](/glossary/#colour). Add a third tincture only if the design needs it. Then keep [the rule of tincture](/guide/tinctures/): metal on colour, colour on metal. The rule exists so that arms can be read at a distance, and nothing makes invented arms look more invented than breaking it by accident.

The maker checks this as you work. When a colour lands on a colour it tells you which layer is at fault and offers to fix it. You may still break the rule on purpose, as Jerusalem did, but then have a story ready for why.

If your nation already has a flag, start from its colours. Arms and flag that share two tinctures look like they belong to the same country.

## Whose arms are they?

Before you draw, decide who bears these arms, because it changes the design.

- **Arms of a dynasty** belong to a family and travel with it. If the House of Maravel loses the throne of Calderwyn, the lion goes with them. These arms tend to be the oldest and simplest.
- **Arms of dominion** belong to the realm, whoever rules it. They often began as a dynasty's arms and stayed behind when the dynasty did not.
- **Arms of a republic** have no ruler to belong to. Many republics kept the old arms and removed the crown. Others chose something new: a civic symbol, a landscape, a tool. A few rejected heraldry altogether and adopted a round emblem, which is itself a statement about the old order.

A realm with a long history may have all three, and the differences between them are history you get for free.

## Arms and the flag

A flag that shows the same design as the shield, stretched to the shape of the flag, is a [banner of arms](/glossary/#banner-of-arms). Medieval rulers flew little else, and several modern flags still are one.

You do not have to match them. Many countries fly a simple flag and keep the arms for seals, coins and embassies. But it helps to design them together. If you have made a flag in [Flagwright](https://flag.eurth.org/), take its colours and its main symbol across. If you are starting here, the shield you make can become the flag.

## Leave room for history

Real arms change. A [bordure](/glossary/#bordure) is added when a younger branch takes the throne; a [chief](/glossary/#chief) appears after a union; a second coat is [quartered](/guide/marshalling/) in. If you design the oldest, plainest version first, you can then work forwards, and every change will have a reason. A shield that carries its past is worth pages of exposition.

```arms
Gules, a lion couchant Or | The first arms of a kingdom
Gules, a lion couchant Or, a bordure argent | After a cadet branch inherits
Gules, a lion couchant Or, on a chief argent three mullets azure, a bordure argent | After a union with three provinces
```

## A checklist

1. One idea, stated in a sentence.
2. One metal and one colour, a third tincture only if needed.
3. The rule of tincture kept, or broken on purpose.
4. Recognisable at thumbnail size.
5. A [blazon](/guide/blazon/) of about ten words or fewer.
6. A reason for every element that someone in your world could give.
